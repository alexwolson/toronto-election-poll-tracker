## Design Specificity Verdict

**Strongly authored for this product.** The warm evidence-grid canvas, Newsreader/Source Sans/IBM Plex Mono hierarchy, ruled data-desk compositions, square geometry, restrained candidate colours, and plain-language probability bands form a coherent civic broadsheet rather than a reusable dashboard skin. The forecast band board and margin distribution are particularly specific to election evidence.

The weakest surfaces are the long record indexes. The 53-candidate roster and trustee grids lean on the visual system without an equally product-specific interaction model. Toronto geography becomes tangible on the maps, but candidate discovery and methodology navigation still behave like generic catalogues.

The deterministic scan reported **239 findings: 8 warnings and 231 advisories**. Of these, 195 concern font-size values outside the compact documented ramp, 35 concern literal colour values, seven flag side rules, one flags a layout transition, and one flags the background grid. Most are not 239 separate visual defects:

- The background grid is an intentional, documented part of the Civic Data Desk direction.
- The side-rule matches mostly encode documented status or editorial structure.
- The layout-transition warning is a false positive on `stroke-width`.
- The font-size findings expose a real maintenance problem: the written four-step type ramp does not describe the many smaller local sizes used in production.
- Literal colours are mixed evidence; several are valid contrast or tint values, but they should be named when they carry reusable meaning.

No reliable user-visible detector overlay is available. Browser security blocked mutable script injection, and the available page evaluation surface is read-only. Rendered screenshot and DOM inspection were used instead.

## Overall Impression

The site already feels credible, careful, and unusually honest about uncertainty. Its biggest opportunity is to **translate evidence for phones and task-oriented visitors instead of preserving desktop density at smaller dimensions**. The visual identity is not the problem; information delivery is.

## What’s Working

1. **The visual language fits the institution.** Firm rules, warm paper, editorial typography, documentary labels, and controlled colour feel authoritative without resembling a government portal template or generic analytics dashboard.

2. **Uncertainty is communicated unusually well.** Frequency phrases, separate forecast and polling routes, labelled status colours, candidate shapes, hatching, and explicit withheld-result language support the product’s evidence commitments.

3. **The interaction foundation is careful.** Skip navigation, visible focus treatment, native disclosures, `aria-pressed`, live result counts, keyboard-selectable map regions, and text paired with colour show deliberate accessibility work.

## Cognitive Load and Emotional Journey

The home page creates the strongest emotional peak: it gives a fast answer in a calm, authoritative frame, then rewards curiosity with evidence. The ward map creates a second peak because it makes local races tangible.

The valleys occur when completeness becomes physical effort:

- `/candidates` presents 53 names in one uninterrupted list with no search, alphabet index, or grouping.
- `/polls` requires horizontal panning on mobile while the user remembers offscreen headers.
- `/how-it-works` reaches roughly 13,600px on a 390px-wide screen and offers seven top-level jump choices only at the beginning.
- The home margin chart compresses historical labels until its most useful context becomes difficult to read.

Decision points exceeding four visible options include five global destinations, seven methodology jump links, 53 candidate choices, 25 ward choices, and 24 poll records. Search and list/map switching mitigate the ward list well; the candidate roster and methodology page do not have comparable relief.

The end of the methodology journey is responsible but emotionally flat. Sources and limitations conclude the page without a clear next step back to a voter’s immediate question.

## Priority Issues

### [P1] Mobile evidence views preserve desktop density instead of translating it

**Why it matters:** Important evidence remains technically present but becomes difficult to read or operate. At 390px, the poll archive is about 591px wide inside a 359px scroller with no visible swipe cue. The margin chart compresses historical labels. Several controls are 32–41px high, and 12 of 25 council map regions measure under 44px in at least one dimension.

**Fix:** Create mobile-specific evidence treatments: a readable margin summary above optional chart detail; poll cards or a sticky first column with a clear “Swipe to see candidate results” cue; minimum 44px primary controls; and enlarged invisible SVG hit areas or a synchronized accessible ward list.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable adapt`

### [P1] The three-candidate polling story lacks enough context to prevent misreading

**Why it matters:** The candidate page says 53 people are on the ballot while the home snapshot, trend, and archive emphasize three. The latest visible shares total 97%, but the remainder, inclusion rule, sample, and survey method are not presented together. A reasonable voter could infer that omitted candidates have zero support or that the three rows are the full reported result.

**Fix:** State the inclusion rule immediately above every three-candidate view. Show the residual as “Other candidates,” “Undecided,” or “Not reported” when the feed supports it. Group pollster, conducted date, sample, survey method, and question base. Add a concise forecast “as of” line beside the lead.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable clarify`

### [P2] The certified candidate roster is complete but difficult to use

**Why it matters:** Fifty-three equally weighted names create a roughly 3,770px mobile scan without search or an alphabet index. A voter who arrives with a name in mind has no efficient route to it. “The 2026 mayoral field” also conflicts with the design language’s own preference for “candidates on the ballot.”

**Fix:** Rename the page “Candidates on the 2026 mayoral ballot.” Add name search, A–Z jump links, and a live visible-result count while preserving neutral alphabetical ordering.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable distill`

### [P2] Methodology is comprehensive but not task-shaped

**Why it matters:** The methodology answers nearly every trust question, but it asks readers to consume the model in the order it operates. The seven jump links help only at the top, and similarly ruled examples weaken the sense of progress over a very long mobile page.

**Fix:** Reframe entry points around voter questions: “Why should I trust the forecast?”, “Why is this ward marked for attention?”, “Why might a result be withheld?”, and “Where does the data come from?” Keep concise answers visible and move technical flows, audit definitions, and full source detail into disclosures. Add a sticky contents control or visible “Back to contents” links.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable distill`

### [P2] Accessibility rigor drops in dense metadata and advanced controls

**Why it matters:** `#888` on the paper background is approximately 3.4:1 and appears in small metadata. Repeated campaign links announced as “Website” or “Candidate link” are ambiguous in a screen-reader link list. The map exposes 25 SVG wards as sequential tab stops rather than a roving spatial control. The detector’s 195 font-size advisories also show that the implemented type system is more fragmented than the documented ramp.

**Fix:** Darken essential metadata, raise meaningful small copy, give campaign links unique accessible names, implement roving focus and arrow-key map navigation while retaining the list fallback, and reconcile the documented type ramp with the sizes the interface truly needs.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable audit`

## Persona Red Flags

**Jordan, first-time voter**

- On `/polls`, “Every public mayoral poll” leads into three candidate series without explaining why those three appear.
- “LOESS trend,” “field,” and sensitivity language require a trip to a long methodology page.
- The 53-name roster offers no first action beyond reading from the top.

**Sam, accessibility-dependent voter**

- The council map creates 25 consecutive SVG tab stops with no arrow-key composite interaction.
- Twelve mobile ward regions fall below 44px in at least one dimension.
- Small faint metadata risks insufficient contrast.
- Repeated “Website” and “Candidate link” labels lose their destination context in a screen-reader link list.

**Casey, distracted mobile voter**

- Global navigation and segmented controls fall below a comfortable 44px touch height.
- The poll archive hides leading candidate columns beyond a horizontal pan with no directional cue.
- The candidate roster and methodology page require thousands of pixels of travel without a persistent resume or section control.
- The margin chart’s historical references become too compressed to scan quickly.

## Minor Observations

- The five-item mobile navigation leaves an empty sixth grid cell. It is usable but visually unfinished.
- Forecast candidate names are not linked to candidate histories, despite that being a likely next question.
- Board abbreviations are compact, but only the current board title expands their meaning.
- Very short trustee pages can end abruptly inside the otherwise generous editorial shell.
- Full-text status tags and legends successfully prevent colour-only interpretation.
- The stored List/Map preference is a thoughtful continuity detail.
- No horizontal page overflow or missing image alt attributes were found on the sampled desktop and mobile routes.

## Questions to Consider

- If a voter sees only the home screen, what single line proves that the forecast and latest poll answer different questions?
- Is showing all 53 candidates in one uninterrupted list preserving neutrality, or converting completeness into invisibility?
- Should a phone reproduce the full chart and table, or translate each into a voter-first summary with detail on demand?
- Could methodology begin with the doubts voters actually have rather than the order in which the model operates?
- What should a voter feel or do after reaching the end of a race, candidate, or methodology page?
